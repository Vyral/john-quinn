A client of ours bought a home, and about six weeks after moving in, the air conditioning started acting up. He had a big company come out to look at it, and their verdict was blunt: the whole unit was shot, rusted, moldy, done. But they could put in a brand new system, ten year warranty, for fifteen thousand dollars. He called me in a panic. Here’s how that fifteen thousand dollar emergency turned into a three hundred and twenty-five dollar fix.

This buyer had only been in the home about six weeks when the AC started having trouble. Some moisture was building up, probably from the humidity, and water was coming out of it. The large company he called told him it was a disaster and quoted a full replacement. Now, he’d just bought the house, and he’s thinking, how am I going to come up with fifteen thousand dollars right now? So he called me, and I had him walk me through what was actually happening. Here’s what I already knew: he’d had a home inspection before buying, and it said the unit was older but fine. So I gave him two independent people to call. One went out, did a few cleanings, worked on a couple of things, and fixed it. It runs perfectly now, and instead of fifteen thousand dollars, it cost him three hundred and twenty-five.

**A shocking bid is not the real price.** It’s just the first number, and very often it’s the worst-case, replace-everything number. I see this constantly. Someone gets a bid that makes their stomach drop and assumes that’s simply what it costs. It isn’t. That AC that was supposedly a fifteen thousand dollar replacement was a three hundred dollar cleaning. And it’s not only air conditioners. I had a seller whose roof was flagged, with a quote of fifteen thousand dollars for a full replacement. One of my independent guys did that same roof, the same shingles, nothing different, for ninety-two hundred. That’s nearly six thousand dollars of difference for the identical job. Just because someone hands you an outrageous number does not mean that’s what you have to pay.

{% include pullquote.html %}

**Once you understand why the big bid happens, you can protect yourself.** Many large outfits have someone whose entire job is to sell you a new system. That’s their role, every single visit, and with heavy overhead to cover, the incentive leans toward replacing rather than repairing. An independent tradesperson is different. The person I send isn’t there to sell you a fifteen thousand dollar unit. He’s there to fix the problem and move on. So protect yourself the simple way: never go with the first bid. Get a second opinion, get a third, and make sure at least one comes from an honest, independent specialist who does just that one thing. I had a homeowner told she needed forty-four windows replaced because the seals were gone, with estimates running twenty-five to forty thousand dollars. I sent someone who does nothing but windows. Two of them actually needed work. She spent about a thousand dollars instead of thirty.

**This is what a good Realtor actually is.** Most people think we’re only there to help you buy or sell, and then we disappear. That isn’t how I work. When that buyer got hit with a fifteen thousand dollar AC bill six weeks after closing, I was the person he called, and I had honest people to point him to. Same with the roof, and the windows. Over the years I’ve built a network of independent, trustworthy tradespeople, and I know who gives an honest estimate and who doesn’t. That value doesn’t end at the closing table. When you own a home, things break and quotes come flying, and it’s easy to be taken advantage of if you don’t know who to trust. Having someone in your corner who does can save you thousands.

So the next time you get a repair bill that makes your heart sink, stop, and don’t just sign it. Get another opinion. Especially in a tight economy, you do not have to accept the first outrageous number someone hands you.
